# Hard Choice Model

A two-by-two matrix that names the kind of decision before you spend effort on it. Published in The Decision Book (Krogerus and Tschäppeler) and by Wes O'Haire (2019). The name refers to Ruth Chang's work on hard choices, but the matrix is not hers.

## Structure

Two questions: how large is the impact of the decision, and how easy is it to compare the options?

| | Easy to compare | Hard to compare |
|---|---|---|
| Low impact | No-brainer | Apples and oranges |
| High impact | Big choice | Hard choice |

## Steps

1. Write the options
2. Rate the impact: what is at stake, for how long, and can the decision be reversed?
3. Rate the comparison: can one scale rank the options? Use Chang's small improvement test. Improve one option a little. If it is still not clearly better than the other option, the options are "on a par" and hard to compare
4. Name the quadrant and respond:
   - No-brainer: decide now
   - Apples and oranges: set a short time limit and pick. The stakes do not justify more
   - Big choice: do the analysis. A better option exists and analysis can find it
   - Hard choice: more analysis does not find a winner. Decide by what you want to stand for, commit, and stop the comparison

## Checks

- Missing information is not "hard to compare". Get the facts first. A choice that looks hard often becomes a big choice
- High stakes do not make a hard choice. The comparison question decides the row
- Do not give a hard choice to a weighted matrix. The weights then hide the decision about values and do not make it
- For a hard choice, do not pick for the user. Show what each option commits them to

## Wrong Tool When

- The options are not yet known: generate options first
- The decision is shared and the parties disagree: use the conflict resolution diagram or principled negotiation
