# Hard Choice Model

A two-by-two matrix that names the kind of decision before you spend effort on it. Wes O'Haire describes it in "Mental Models for Designers", and a similar matrix is in The Decision Book (Krogerus and Tschäppeler). The name refers to Ruth Chang's work on hard choices, but the matrix is not hers.

## Structure

Two questions: how large is the impact of the decision, and how easy is it to compare the options?

| | Easy to compare | Hard to compare |
|---|---|---|
| Low impact | No-brainer | Apples and oranges |
| High impact | Big choice | Hard choice |

## Steps

1. Write the options
2. Rate the impact: what is at stake, for how long, and can the decision be reversed?
3. Rate the comparison: can one scale rank the options? A test from Chang helps. Improve one option a little. If it is still not clearly better than the other option, the options are "on a par" and hard to compare
4. Name the quadrant
5. Choose the response. These responses are suggestions; the matrix itself gives only the four names:
   - No-brainer: decide now. More effort is waste
   - Apples and oranges: decide by what is important to you at this time, with a short time limit
   - Big choice: collect the information and compare with care. A better option exists and analysis can find it
   - Hard choice: accept that no option is the correct one. Reduce the risk with a small experiment where you can. Then decide by what you want to stand for, and commit

## Checks

- Missing information is not "hard to compare". Get the facts first. A choice that looks hard often becomes a big choice
- High stakes do not make a hard choice. The comparison question decides the column
- A weighted matrix can put the factors of a hard choice in order, but the weights are the value decision. Show them as the user's choice, not as a result
- For a hard choice, do not pick for the user. Show what each option commits them to

## Wrong Tool When

- The options are not yet known: generate options first
- The decision is shared and the parties disagree: use the conflict resolution diagram or principled negotiation
